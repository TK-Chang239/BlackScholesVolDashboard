### 2026-08-27 — The pricing formula

The Black-Scholes formula prices a European option as the discounted expected payoff under the risk-neutral measure, where both the spot and strike are discounted at their appropriate rates. The key terms are d1 and d2: d2 represents the risk-neutral probability that the option finishes in-the-money under the stock's forward distribution, while d1 is d2 shifted by σ√T, reflecting the difference in measuring probabilities under two numeraires (the bond vs the stock). Concretely, a call price is `S e^{-qT} N(d1) - K e^{-rT} N(d2)`, where the spot discount `e^{-qT}` appears naturally from the continuous dividend yield q; the test `test_dividend_yield_is_spot_shift` validates this by proving that BSM(S, q) is exactly equivalent to pricing with an effective spot `S e^{-qT}` and zero dividend, showing that dividend yield acts purely as a forward adjustment.

Put-call parity, `C - P = S e^{-qT} - K e^{-rT}`, holds to machine epsilon here by algebraic construction: since both calls and puts are built from the same d1 and d2 values and discount factors, their difference cancels all the Normal-CDF terms and reduces to the forward-strike identity. In real markets, parity typically only holds to the bid-ask spread because of transaction costs, market fragmentation, and discrete settlement; our implementation achieves machine-precision parity (validated in `TestIdentities`) because there are no such frictions. This distinction clarifies that parity is not a robust market law but an artifact of frictionless pricing.

As volatility approaches zero, the option price converges to the discounted intrinsic value of the forward—not the spot—which emerges naturally from the limiting behavior of d1 and d2. When σ → 0, both d1 and d2 approach negative infinity for out-of-the-money options (shrinking the CDF to zero) or positive infinity for in-the-money options (shrinking the CDF to one), causing the price to flatten to `max(S e^{-qT} - K e^{-rT}, 0)` for calls. This forward-intrinsic limit, validated in `TestBoundaries`, shows why options have real value even at zero vol when the forward is above strike; the discount factors and dividend yield are essential to the boundary behavior, not incidental.

The implementation safeguards underflow in the denominator (σ√T) with a small epsilon guard and uses NumPy broadcasting to handle scalar and array inputs uniformly. Invalid numeric inputs (negative spot, strike, or time; negative or infinite volatility) yield NaN elementwise rather than raising, allowing the downstream pipeline to track data quality through status counters. This "NaN-never-raise" contract preserves vectorization and handles bad market data gracefully without breaking batch processing.

### 2026-08-27 — The Greeks

The five Greeks capture how option prices change with moves in the underlying market variables. **Delta** (∂P/∂S) measures the option's sensitivity to the stock price—a call's delta equals `e^{-qT} N(d1)` and a put's equals `e^{-qT} (N(d1) - 1)`, both of which lie between 0 and 1 for calls and between -1 and 0 for puts. **Gamma** (∂²P/∂S²) is the rate of change of delta with respect to spot; it is always positive for both calls and puts (reflected in the formula as `e^{-qT} φ(d1) / (S σ √T)`) and represents the curvature of the price surface. **Vega** (∂P/∂σ) measures sensitivity to volatility and equals `S e^{-qT} φ(d1) √T`; like gamma, it is always positive. **Theta** (∂P/∂t) is the time decay per **year**; its sign depends on moneyness and whether the position is a call or put (for long options it is typically negative, meaning time decay erodes value). **Rho** (∂P/∂r) captures interest-rate sensitivity and differs between calls and puts: a call's rho is positive (`K T e^{-rT} N(d2)`), while a put's is negative.

A classic interview trap asks why an at-the-money call delta sits slightly above 0.5 rather than exactly at 0.5. The answer lies in the drift term in d1: `d1 = (ln(S/K) + (r - q + σ²/2) T) / (σ √T)`. When S = K at-the-money, d1 = `(r - q + σ²/2) √T / σ` is positive (assuming r > q and positive vol), which means N(d1) > 0.5. This drift—the excess of the risk-free rate over the dividend yield, plus half the variance—pushes the forward above the strike, raising the probability of finishing in-the-money slightly. This same r−q+σ²/2 drift is one of the trickier concepts in interview questions (questions 1 and 6 in the guide) because it is not the stock's actual drift (which is r under the risk-neutral measure) but the forward's drift relative to the discounted spot.

Both gamma and vega share the probability-density-function kernel φ(d1), which is why both peak at-the-money and decay toward zero deep in-the-money or out-of-the-money. This shared geometry is more than notational accident: gamma measures the sensitivity of the hedge ratio (delta) to moves in the stock, while vega measures the sensitivity of price to moves in volatility expectations. Both are largest when the option is at-the-money because that is where small moves in the underlying have the largest fractional impact on the probability of finishing in-the-money (the maximum slope of the CDF N(d1)). At-the-money options are the most "gamma-rich" and "vega-rich," which is why short-selling at-the-money options to collect time decay (selling theta) is most dangerous there—the buyer of that protection is buying the most convex exposure. This insight seeds question 6, but not as a theory of the smile itself: because vega decays toward zero in the wings, any procedure that inverts price into vol—Newton, Brent, a broker's own solver—becomes numerically fragile exactly where the smile is most interesting to read. The geometry explains why wing IVs are noisy to recover, not why they're higher in the first place.

The theta convention chosen here is ∂P/∂t per **year**, not per day. This is mathematically clean (the formulas differentiate T directly) but requires the render layer to scale theta for human display—dividing by 365 to show per-day decay. The brief explicitly marks this as a raw derivative; downstream pricing feeds and dashboards must handle the scaling. For a long call, theta is negative (time decay erodes the option's value toward expiry), while for a short call theta is positive profit. The formula accounts for both the decay of the Gaussian term and the changing discount factors as T shrinks.

Finally, differentiating put-call parity with respect to spot yields the delta-parity relation: `delta_call - delta_put = e^{-qT}`. This holds exactly in our implementation (validated in `TestShapes`) because delta for a call is `e^{-qT} N(d1)` and for a put is `e^{-qT} (N(d1) - 1)`, so their difference is exactly `e^{-qT}`. This relation is important for market-making: if you are short one call and long one put at the same strike and expiry, your net delta exposure is `-e^{-qT}` (a short position in the forward discounted at the dividend yield). This follows algebraically from parity but often surprises practitioners who confuse delta with the more intuitive notion of "how many shares behave like this option."

### 2026-08-27 — Inverting the formula: implied vol

Implied vol only makes sense as a concept because `bs_price` is strictly increasing in σ — `TestBoundaries::test_monotone_increasing_in_vol` from Task 3 is the proof, and it's the whole reason inversion is well-posed rather than a guessing game. A strictly monotone continuous function from `(0, ∞)` onto `(intrinsic, upper_bound)` has a unique inverse on that range, so "the σ that produces this price" is a well-defined single number, not a family of candidates — provided the price actually falls in the reachable range. The Newton step needs a starting point, and the Brenner–Subrahmanyam guess `√(2π/T) · price/S` comes from linearizing the ATM price formula: near the money, `C ≈ 0.4 · S · σ√T` (the constant is `1/√(2π) ≈ 0.3989`), so solving for σ gives `σ ≈ √(2π/T) · C/S`. It's derived for ATM options specifically, which is why the docstring calls it "near-exact ATM, decent start elsewhere" — everywhere else it's just a reasonable place to start Newton, not a real estimate.

Question 7 asks what actually breaks Newton deep OTM, and I hit the real failure rather than a hypothetical one. The naive first draft checked convergence (`|diff| < tol`) *before* checking whether vega was too small to trust. For `test_deep_otm_tiny_vega_does_not_return_garbage` (S=100, K=300, T=0.05, true σ=0.30), the true price is ~1.4e-60 — so small it's indistinguishable from 0 in float64. The Brenner–Subrahmanyam guess for that price collapses to the `_MIN_VOL` floor, and at `_MIN_VOL` the computed price is *also* 0.0 in float64, so `diff = -1.4e-60`, which is comfortably under `tol=1e-10`. The loop declared victory on iteration one and returned σ = 1e-4 — a plausible-looking number that was completely wrong, exactly the "silently wrong" outcome the corner contract forbids. The fix was a one-line reorder: check `vega < floor` and release to Brent *before* evaluating convergence, so a near-zero-vega point can never be accepted just because its price happens to round to the target.

That fix didn't fully solve it, though, which is the more interesting failure. Because `tol` lives in price space, the resulting σ error is only ever ~`tol/vega` — reasonable off-ATM, but the docstring's own claim implies a lower bound on how small vega can be before the guaranteed accuracy evaporates. I'd set the "vega is too small to trust" cutoff to a bare `1e-10`, matching the underflow guard already used elsewhere in the file — but a point with vega = 8.8e-10 sailed past that gate, "converged" on a diff of 5.4e-11, and returned σ = 3.15 for a true σ of 3.00 (a K=300, T=1-day put): an error of 0.15, three orders of magnitude past the 1e-6 promise. `tol/vega ≈ 1e-10/8.8e-10 ≈ 0.11` — the docstring's own formula predicted this exact failure once I did the arithmetic; the cutoff just wasn't derived from the accuracy target it was supposed to protect. The real gate has to be `vega ≥ tol / target_accuracy = 1e-10 / 1e-6 = 1e-4`, not an arbitrary underflow-sized number — I checked this against the normal grid's minimum vega (~0.0068) to confirm the tighter floor never falsely disqualifies a healthy point.

Brent can't fail to find a root that mathematically exists inside a bracket with a genuine sign change — that's the whole content of the intermediate value theorem, and it's why it's the designated fallback instead of a second Newton variant. But "genuine sign change" is doing real work in that sentence: for the deepest, shortest-dated corners (K=300 vs S=100, T=1 day), `bs_price(σ)` is *bit-identical* to the target price across almost the entire domain from `_MIN_VOL` up to σ≈3, and only diverges past that. Brent, searching for where the float64-evaluated sign flips, correctly converges to the edge of that plateau — not to whatever σ the test happened to generate the price from, because within the plateau every σ is an equally valid "root" of the floating-point function. I added a post-check mirroring the Newton fix: compute vega at whatever Brent returns, and discard the root (leave NaN) if it's below the same `vega_floor` derived above, rather than trust a technically-valid root that carries no real information. This is question 6's answer applied to the solver rather than the pricer: vega's peak-at-the-money, decays-to-zero-in-the-wings geometry (Task 4) isn't a quirk of this codebase, it's why *any* numerical inversion — ours, a broker's, a Bloomberg terminal's — gets noisy or undefined in the deep wings. The flatness is in the option's economics, not in our loop, which is also the argument for why no-arbitrage violations return NaN immediately rather than a clamped σ: a price above the upper bound or below intrinsic isn't "a vol we solved slightly wrong," it's a price that no σ could have produced, so *any* number returned there is fabricated. Clamping it to `_MAX_VOL` or `_MIN_VOL` would look like a real answer downstream — the smile panel would happily plot a fictitious point next to real ones, and nothing would flag it as garbage. NaN is the only value that's honest about "no σ answers this," the same discipline as the `_invalid_mask` in Tasks 3–4: bad inputs don't get a best-effort number, they get a value the caller is forced to check for.

### 2026-08-28 — Realized volatility: measuring what happened

**Why log returns.** `log_returns` computes `np.log(adjusted_close).diff()`, i.e. r_t = ln(P_t / P_{t−1}), and the reason is additivity in time. The log return over two days is exactly the sum of the two daily log returns; simple percentage returns don't have that property (+10% then −10% is −1%, not 0%). Volatility is the standard deviation of returns over a horizon, and the √-time scaling that lets a daily number be quoted annually only holds when the underlying quantities add. It also matches the model being tested: Black-Scholes assumes ln(S_T/S_0) is normal, so the log return is precisely the quantity whose σ the formula is asking about. Measuring something else and calling it "realized vol" would be putting a different statistic next to the model's σ and pretending they are comparable.

**Why *adjusted* close.** SPY pays a quarterly distribution in the $1.80–$2.00 range (measured directly from EODHD's `div/SPY.US` in the Phase 0 entry above); on the ex-dividend morning the raw close drops by roughly that amount. That drop is a cash transfer, not a price move — nobody lost money. Feeding raw closes into a vol estimate books four fake ~0.25% down-moves a year, and books them as isolated outliers rather than as part of the return distribution, which is exactly what a squared-deviation estimator over-weights. `test_uses_adjusted_close_not_close` pins this with the cleanest possible case: a synthetic series whose adjusted close is flat at 100.0 for 30 days while the raw close steps from 100.0 to 98.0 halfway through. The correct answer is exactly zero realized vol, and the test asserts `np.allclose(rv.dropna(), 0.0)`. If the function ever reached for `close` instead of `adjusted_close`, that one 2% step would produce a nonzero number and the test would fail — a dividend would be indistinguishable from a crash.

**Why `ddof=1` and why √252.** The rolling std uses `ddof=1` — the Bessel correction — because the mean being subtracted is itself estimated from the same 20 returns, not known in advance. Dividing by n instead of n−1 systematically understates the variance by (n−1)/n; at a 20-day window that is a 5% variance bias, roughly 2.5% in vol, which is not nothing when the entire spread the dashboard is trying to show is 1–2 vol points wide. `test_alternating_returns_match_closed_form` encodes it exactly: for 20 alternating ±x returns the expected value is x·√(20/19)·√252, and that √(20/19) factor *is* `ddof=1` written out. The √252 is the annualization — variance scales linearly with elapsed time, so vol scales with its square root, and there are about 252 trading days in a year. 252 rather than 365 because a weekend produces one return, not three: the estimator counts observations, not calendar days.

**Trailing vs forward, and today's numbers.** `trailing_realized_vol` is a backward-looking rolling window — at date t it reports the vol of the last `window` returns ending at t. `forward_realized_vol` is the mirror image and is the more useful one for scoring the model: for each date t it collects the returns strictly after t through t + 30 *calendar* days, requires at least `MIN_FORWARD_RETURNS = 5` of them, and returns NaN otherwise — including for every date whose window has not fully matured, with the loop `break`ing once t+30 passes the last stored date because nothing later can be matured either (`test_matured_dates_only` asserts the NaN pattern is exactly "matured or not"). That is the honest forecast target: a 30-DTE implied vol quoted on day t is a claim about the 30 days *after* t, so grading it against trailing RV grades it against data it already had. `test_value_matches_hand_computation` rebuilds one date's forward window by hand — including the close on t itself as the base of the first return — and matches to 1e-9, while `test_too_few_returns_is_nan` confirms a 3-day horizon (at most 3 returns) never produces a number at all. Today, 2026-08-28, SPY's trailing vols are **rv_20d 10.40%** and **rv_60d 13.96%** — a wide gap saying the last month has been meaningfully calmer than the quarter, since rv_20d has fallen from 13.33% on 2026-08-14 while rv_60d has barely moved (13.93% → 13.96%). Across the whole rebuilt history exactly **one** row carries a matured forward RV: 2024-09-03, at **11.15%**. Every other stored session is less than 30 days old. That single number is the entire forecast scorecard so far, and the P5 entry below is honest about what one point can and cannot support.

### 2026-08-28 — Sensitivity: which inputs matter

**The run.** P1 prices one option — a call struck at spot, S = 769.35, at the 30-day ATM implied vol the pipeline had just solved (σ = 11.8324%), T = 30/365, r = 3.832%, q = 0.9781% — worth **$11.316** — then bumps each of the five inputs by the same ±20% and reports the price change. Today's tornado:

- **σ (volatility):** −18.31% / +18.33% — span **36.63%**
- **T (time to expiry):** −11.31% / +10.38% — span **21.69%**
- **r (risk-free rate):** −2.21% / +2.24% — span **4.45%**
- **q (dividend yield):** +0.58% / −0.58% — span **1.17%**
- **S (spot):** −100.00% / +1274.58% — span **1374.58%**

**Why σ's response is almost exactly linear, and why "almost".** At the money the price is dominated by the vol term from the Brenner-Subrahmanyam linearization already used by the solver: C ≈ (1/√(2π))·S·σ·√T = 0.3989 × 769.35 × 0.118324 × √(30/365) = **$10.412**, which is **92.0%** of the $11.316 price. If the price were *purely* that term, a +20% σ bump would give exactly +20%. Instead it gives +18.33% — and 20% × 92.0% = 18.40%, which is the observed number to within 0.07 points. The missing 8% of the price is the forward-drift piece: the 30-day forward is F = S·e^{(r−q)T} = 771.16, so a call struck at 769.35 is already $1.81 in the money on the forward and carries roughly ½·(F−K)·e^{−rT} ≈ $0.90 that doesn't scale with σ at all. The down-bump, −18.31%, is within 0.02 points of the mirror image of the up-bump, and that near-symmetry is the statement that vomma (∂²P/∂σ²) is essentially zero at the money — the same geometric fact that makes the Brenner-Subrahmanyam guess "near-exact ATM" in the implied-vol entry above. `test_sigma_dominates_the_argued_group_and_rate_is_tiny` codifies the tolerance directly: σ's `up_pct` must equal 0.20 to within ±0.03.

**Why r and q barely register at 30 days.** Two things compound. First, a ±20% bump of a small number is a small number: 20% of r = 3.832% is ±0.766 percentage points of rate, and 20% of q = 0.978% is ±0.196 pp. Second, at 30 days those rates barely have time to act — rT ≈ 0.0031, so the strike discount e^{−rT} ≈ 0.99685, and moving r by 0.766 pp moves that factor by about 6 basis points. The result is that r's whole ±20% span moves the price 4.45% and q's moves it 1.17%, against σ's 36.63% — σ's span is **8.2×** r's and **31.4×** q's. Note also that q's sign is inverted (a higher dividend yield *lowers* a call, because q discounts the spot leg via e^{−qT}), which is why its bar points the opposite way. The practical statement, which is the whole answer to Q1: a desk quoting a one-month option that gets the rate wrong by a fifth of itself misprices by 2%; getting the vol wrong by a fifth misprices by 18%. Which is why desks quote vol, not price.

**The documented deviation from SPEC §3 P1.** The SPEC's literal acceptance for this panel is "σ dominates." On real numbers it doesn't — S's span (1374.58%) is **37.5×** σ's (36.63%), because a 20% move in a 769 spot is $154 against an $11 option. Putting all five bars on one axis renders σ, T, r and q as invisible slivers next to a bar 37 times longer, which teaches exactly the wrong lesson. So `compute_sensitivity` tags every input with a `group`: σ, r and q are **argued** (a trader estimates them and the market can disagree), while S and T are **observed** (nobody disagrees about the spot print or the calendar — a 20% move in spot is a crash, not a difference of opinion). `build_sensitivity_figure` then gives each group its own subplot and its own axis, so σ's dominance *within the group where dominance is meaningful* is visible instead of crushed. Two tests keep both halves honest: `test_sigma_dominates_the_argued_group_and_rate_is_tiny` (σ span > 5× r's and > 5× q's, r span < 0.05) and `test_spot_is_the_observed_giant` (S's span exceeds σ's, and a 20% crash takes the call to ≈ −100%). That last assertion is very nearly literal, though not numerically exact, and the difference is worth being precise about: at S = 615.48 the 30-day call struck at spot prices to **1.297e-10** — about a hundred-millionth of a cent — so it *rounds* to $0.000000 at six decimals and `down_pct` comes out −0.9999999999885, not exactly −1. The −100% bar is display rounding of a value that is economically annihilated but numerically still positive, which is also why `test_spot_is_the_observed_giant` asserts −1.0 with an `abs=0.02` tolerance rather than exact equality. A 20% crash doesn't make this call cheap; it takes it to a price no one would bother to quote.

### 2026-08-28 — Greeks in practice

**Today's tiles.** The panel picks the stored expiry nearest 30 DTE — 2026-09-18, 21 days out — and within it the converged strike nearest spot, K = 769 against spot 769.35, evaluating each leg at its own market IV:

- **call**, IV 10.85%: delta **+0.537**, gamma **0.0198**, vega **+0.733** per vol point, theta **−0.221** per day, rho **+0.233** per 1% of rate
- **put**, IV 12.14%: delta **−0.465**, gamma **0.0177**, vega **+0.733**, theta **−0.183**, rho **−0.211**

**Display scaling, and why the model layer refuses to do it.** Those per-day, per-vol-point and per-1% numbers are not what `greeks()` returns. The model layer returns raw calculus derivatives — theta per *year*, vega per *unit* of vol, rho per *unit* of rate — and `src/render/tiles.py` divides by 365, 100 and 100 respectively at display time. Three reasons the division lives there and not in the model. First, a derivative has exactly one true value, while per-day and per-vol-point are presentation conventions and not even universal ones (plenty of desks divide theta by 252 rather than 365, counting trading days). Second, the identities the model is tested against are stated in raw units: `test_theta_is_calendar_derivative` checks theta against a finite difference in T, a check that is only meaningful per year — scale inside `greeks()` and the test must either un-scale or quietly change meaning. Third, parity relations only hold in raw units. `test_display_scaling` pins the divisions exactly where they belong by feeding theta = −36.5, vega = 95.0 and rho = 31.0 into the renderer and asserting the HTML contains "-0.100", "0.950" and "0.310".

**Delta above a half — and a parity gap that isn't a bug.** The ATM call delta is e^{−qT}N(d₁) = 0.537, above 0.5 for the reason the earlier Greeks entry set out: d₁ = (ln(S/K) + (r−q+σ²/2)T)/(σ√T) is positive whenever the forward sits above the strike, and here it does twice over — K = 769 is $0.35 below spot, and the r−q+σ²/2 drift adds more on top. The e^{−qT} factor (0.99944 at 21 days) shaves off only a hair. Now the interesting part. `test_put_call_delta_parity` asserts Δ_c − Δ_p = e^{−qT} to 1e-9, but today's tiles give 0.536983 − (−0.465242) = **1.002225** against e^{−qT} = **0.999437** — off by +0.002788. That is not a broken identity. Parity is a statement about one option pair evaluated at *one* σ, and the tiles deliberately evaluate each leg at its own market IV: the K=769 put trades at 12.14% while the call trades at 10.85%. Feeding either of those two vols to *both* legs reproduces e^{−qT} to the last digit (checked this session at 0.108487 and at 0.121392 — both give 0.9994374168). So the 2.8e-3 residual is the **1.29-vol-point skew** between the put and the call at the same strike, arriving in the delta column as a number that would be exactly zero in a world where Black-Scholes were true. It is P2's crash premium showing up somewhere P2 doesn't draw it.

**Gamma peaks near the forward, not at spot — and the skew nudges it further out.** The curves evaluate all 233 call strikes and 246 put strikes of the 2026-09-18 expiry, each at its own IV. Call gamma maxes at **K = 774** and put gamma at **K = 776**, with spot at 769.35. Two effects stack. ### 2026-08-29 — 25-delta skew: why interpolate in delta

**What a 25-delta option is, and why it is the same contract in every regime.** Delta is the option's sensitivity to spot, ∂P/∂S, and for an out-of-the-money option it doubles as a rough risk-neutral probability of finishing in the money: a put with delta −0.25 is, to first order, "the put that pays off on a roughly one-in-four down-move." That description is the point. A *strike* is not comparable across days — the 750 put is 2.5% out of the money when SPY is 769 and 12% out of the money when SPY is 850, and it is a different insurance contract in a 10% vol regime than in a 30% one, because vol sets how far the distribution reaches. Delta normalises both at once: it already contains spot, time and the option's own volatility through d₁, so pinning delta pins the *economic* contract — one-in-four down-move protection — and lets you difference it against its mirror image on the call side. `compute_skew_25d` therefore takes each row's own market IV, computes that row's delta with this project's `greeks()`, and interpolates IV as a function of delta with `np.interp`, restricting the put side to strikes below spot and the call side to strikes at or above it so the number always comes off the out-of-the-money wings. `test_interpolates_at_delta_not_strike` is the test that makes this more than a naming choice: it builds a chain with a known vol-by-strike function, independently recomputes the strike K\* whose own-IV delta is −0.25, and asserts that `put_iv_25d` equals `skew_fn(K*)` to within 0.003 — that is, the returned IV must be the vol at the delta-located strike, not at some strike picked by a rule of thumb.

**Today's number.** The panel picks the stored expiry nearest the 30-day target, which today is 2026-09-18 at **21 DTE**, with spot at 769.35 and a $1-wide strike ladder (233 call strikes and 246 put strikes converge). On the put side the −0.25 delta falls between K = 755 (δ = −0.2590, IV 13.553%) and K = 754 (δ = −0.2479, IV 13.677%); the interpolation lands on **put_iv_25d = 13.653%** at an effective strike of 754.19, **1.97% below spot**. On the call side +0.25 falls between K = 783 (δ = +0.2489, IV 9.647%) and K = 782 (δ = +0.2682, IV 9.713%), giving **call_iv_25d = 9.650%** at an effective strike of 782.94, **1.77% above spot**. The skew is the difference: **skew_25d = +4.003 vol points**. Read that plainly. The two contracts sit almost mirror-image distances from spot — $15.2 below versus $13.6 above — and the market charges the downside one a volatility a third higher than the upside one. That is the crash premium, priced.

**Why Black-Scholes says it should be zero, and what the history shows.** Black-Scholes has exactly one σ; it prices every strike and both kinds off the same lognormal distribution. Invert prices generated that way and every point on the chain returns the same number, so put_iv(−25Δ) − call_iv(+25Δ) is identically zero — that is not an approximation but an algebraic consequence, and `test_flat_vol_has_zero_skew_at_nearest_expiry` pins it by building a flat-0.22 chain and asserting both wings come back at 0.22 with |skew| < 1e-9. On real data it is never zero. After `scripts/rebuild_daily_metrics.py` filled the column for the whole archive, **11 of the 12** stored sessions carry a finite skew, ranging **+3.43 vol points** (2026-08-26) to **+4.91** (2026-08-20), mean **+4.11**. It is positive on every single day. (As first shipped all 12 were finite, the twelfth being 2024-09-03's **+6.08** — the largest reading in the sample, and read off a **17-day** expiry against a 30-day target. The final review of the branch made `compute_skew_25d` honour the same ±10-day maturity tolerance `interp_atm_iv` already used, so that session now records NaN rather than a number of a different tenor. Losing the sample's maximum is the point, not a cost: see the paragraph below, which had already measured that the maturity effect is the same size as the signal.) `test_put_skew_is_positive_and_bracketed_by_neighbours` keeps the sign convention honest (skew > 0.01 on a synthetic put-skewed chain, with the call side flat at 0.20), and `test_unbracketed_side_is_nan` guarantees the number is never fabricated: if the chain's deltas do not straddle the target, `_interp_iv_at_delta` returns NaN rather than extrapolating off the end of the ladder.

**What's ugly — two confounds sitting inside a single scalar.** First, the maturity is not constant. `skew_dte` records the *actual* expiry used, and as first shipped it ran **17 to 35 days** across the 12 stored sessions (17, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 35). Unlike the ATM term structure, which interpolates to a true 30-day point, skew has to live on one real expiry — you cannot interpolate a delta-space wing measurement across maturities without re-deriving the deltas — so the series compared a 17-day skew against a 35-day one. That matters because skew is itself maturity-dependent: the model-vs-market entry below measures the mean OTM-put vol premium falling monotonically from **+20.98** vol points at 21 DTE to **+13.16** at 203 DTE on today's chain alone. So the series' maximum, 2024-09-03's +6.08, sat on the shortest maturity in the sample (17 days), and some unknown part of it was the maturity, not the regime. **That observation is the whole argument for a bound, and this entry made it without acting on it.** The `atm_iv_30d` column sitting in the same row already refused any expiry more than 10 days from the target; skew did not, so one row could honestly report `atm_iv_30d = NaN` beside a `skew_25d` read off the very expiry the ATM reader had just rejected. The branch's final review closed that: `compute_skew_25d` now imports `ATM_NEAREST_TOLERANCE_DAYS` from `daily_metrics` and returns empty beyond it. The stored range is now **21 to 35 days** over 11 sessions and 2024-09-03 is NaN — the only row affected, confirmed by replaying every stored chain. A shorter series that measures one thing beats a longer one that splices two. Second, and worse for today's specific number: the put IV comes from puts and the call IV from calls, and at this front expiry the two are not consistent with each other. Averaged over the strikes within 2% of spot, the *same-strike* put IV exceeds the call IV by **+1.54 vol points** (median +1.32) — a gap that put-call parity says must be exactly zero. At 112 and 140 DTE the same measurement gives **−0.04** points, i.e. zero. So of today's 4.00-point skew, on the order of a vol point may be the front-expiry forward discrepancy dissected in the next entry rather than genuine wing asymmetry. The number is not wrong — it is the correct summary of the quotes we have — but it is measuring two things at once, and only one of them is skew.

### 2026-08-29 — Put-call parity: the model-free check

**The derivation, and why no volatility appears.** Build two portfolios. Portfolio A: buy one call struck at K, sell one put struck at K, same expiry T, and hold K·e^{−rT} of cash. At expiry the cash has grown to K, the call pays max(S_T − K, 0) and the short put pays −max(K − S_T, 0); those two option legs together pay exactly S_T − K in *every* state of the world (above K the call pays S_T − K and the put expires worthless; below K the call expires worthless and the short put costs K − S_T), so portfolio A is worth S_T. Portfolio B: buy e^{−qT} shares and reinvest the continuous dividend stream back into the stock; by T that holding has compounded to exactly one share, worth S_T. Two portfolios with identical payoffs in every state must cost the same today, or you sell the dear one, buy the cheap one, and hold a locked box to expiry. Hence **C − P = S·e^{−qT} − K·e^{−rT}**. Notice what is absent: no σ, no N(d₁), no lognormal assumption, no assumption about the distribution of S_T at all. The argument used only that the payoffs match state by state, which is why parity survives every criticism aimed at Black-Scholes — fat tails, stochastic vol, jumps — and why it is the right instrument for checking whether the *data* is sane before blaming the model. `compute_parity` computes both sides per strike/expiry pair, and `test_synthetic_prices_satisfy_parity_to_machine_eps` confirms that prices generated by our own `bs_price` satisfy it to 1e-9, as they must by the algebra of the previous entry on the formula.

**Tradeable violations, and the exit criterion missed.** A dollar gap is not an arbitrage. To harvest a deviation you must cross both bid–ask spreads — buy the cheap leg at the ask, sell the dear leg at the bid — so `compute_parity` marks `tradeable_violation` only when |deviation| exceeds the *combined* spread of the two legs, `(call_ask − call_bid) + (put_ask − put_bid)`, and only when that spread is known at all. Close-based sessions have no quotes, so `spread` is NaN and the flag is unconditionally False; `test_close_based_rows_have_no_spread_and_never_trade` pins that, and the panel's stat line says so in words rather than reporting a comforting zero. `test_violation_beyond_spread_is_flagged` and `test_deviation_inside_spread_is_not_tradeable` pin the threshold from both sides. Today's live run gives **654 strike/expiry pairs, all quoted, with 132 tradeable violations — 20.2%**, and a maximum absolute deviation of **$160.28**. The Phase 5 exit criterion was "≈ 0 tradeable violations." It is not met, and the honest reading is that the check is working: it is finding real inconsistencies in the quote feed, not free money. Split the 654 pairs by open interest and the picture separates cleanly. Where **both** legs have open interest above zero — 585 pairs — the violation rate is **14.9%** (87 pairs). Where at least one leg has zero open interest — 69 pairs — it is **65.2%** (45 pairs). The single worst cell is the 2027-01-15 K = 910 pair: the call is quoted 0.41/0.42 with 4,638 contracts of open interest, while the put is quoted **288.50/293.39** against an intrinsic value of only $140.65, on zero open interest and one contract of volume. Our own solver returns an implied vol of **109.2%** for it. That is not an American-exercise premium; it is a stale or placeholder quote from a contract nobody trades, and it alone sets the heatmap's colour range at ±$160.

**Where the real violations are — and it is not where the caption says.** The P7 caption attributes residual deep-in-the-money put gaps to American early exercise, and the shape of the data is consistent with that story in the sense that deep-ITM puts are indeed where the gaps concentrate: of the 50 pairs with moneyness ≥ 1.10, **48 violate**, with a median |deviation| of **$9.10** — but only 8 of those 50 have open interest on both legs, so the early-exercise explanation and the dead-quote explanation are entangled and this session cannot separate them. The genuinely interesting population is the other one. In the 0.97–1.03 moneyness band, **54 of 127** pairs violate, with a median combined spread of just **$1.54** against a median |deviation| of **$0.98**. Narrow to the front expiry at 0.99–1.01 moneyness and **13 of 16** pairs violate with a median combined spread of **$0.345** and a median deviation of **−$0.976** — one-signed, not scattered. These are the most liquid contracts on the board: the 2026-09-18 K = 770 call is quoted 8.18/8.24 on 4,767 contracts of volume and the matching put 8.55/8.59 on 10,187, and their C − P sits **$0.97 below** what S·e^{−qT} − K·e^{−rT} demands. Nothing about that is illiquidity. Something in our inputs, or in the vendor's timestamps, is off by about a dollar of forward.

**Backing out the carry — and what it says about the missing dollar.** Set K = S in parity and it rearranges into a forward: C − P = S·e^{−qT} − K·e^{−rT} implies **F = K + (C − P)·e^{rT}**, and since parity holds at every strike, each near-the-money strike gives that same F directly, with no interpolation at all. `implied_carry` takes the median of those readings per expiry (it interpolated between the two ATM strikes when this entry was first written; the third follow-up entry explains why that was replaced) and reports ln(F/S)/T, which under the model must equal **r − q**; `test_recovers_r_minus_q` confirms it recovers R − Q to 1e-8 on parity-exact synthetic prices, and `test_expiry_not_bracketing_spot_is_skipped` refuses to extrapolate when the strike ladder does not straddle spot. On today's chain the six expiries give **0.65% (21d), 1.78% (49d), 2.83% (84d), 2.87% (112d), 2.86% (140d), 3.23% (203d)** against our r − q = **2.854%**. The three middle maturities land within **3 basis points**; the page as Phase 5 first shipped it, however, reported the ~30-day target, which resolves to the 21-day expiry, so the stat line read "ATM implied carry at the 21-day expiry: 0.65% vs our r − q = 2.85%" — a 220-basis-point miss. Converted back into dollars the discrepancy is small and almost identical at the two front expiries: the implied forward is **$0.98 low at 21 days and $1.11 low at 49 days**, versus **−$0.05, +$0.04, +$0.01 and +$1.65** at 84, 112, 140 and 203 days. Carry divides that dollar error by T, so the same $1 becomes 2.2 vol-of-rate points at three weeks and nothing at three months — the reported carry number is far more fragile than the forward it comes from, and quoting it at the shortest expiry is the least informative choice available. As for the missing dollar: the leading candidate is the one named in private question 9 — SPY pays lumpy quarterly dividends and we model a continuous q, and the very next ex-dividend date is 2026-09-18, the front expiry itself. A continuous q of 0.978% accrues only **$0.43** of dividend over 21 days where one discrete quarterly payout is about **$1.88**, a $1.45 gap of the right sign and order. But that hypothesis predicts a −$0.15 error at 84 days and a −$0.88 error at 140 days, and the data shows −$0.08 and −$0.05, so it does not reproduce the whole term pattern. This is not settled, and I am not going to pretend it is. The near-ATM combined spreads at 84–203 days are $0.44–$1.71, tight enough that a $1 forward error would have shown up there and did not, so it is not simply measurement noise either. What Phase 5 has produced is a sharp, reproducible, dollar-denominated statement of the problem — which is exactly the input question 9 needs.

### 2026-08-29 — Model vs market: the smile in price space

**The exercise.** P9 takes the model at its word. Black-Scholes assumes one volatility governs the whole underlying, so `compute_model_vs_market` prices **every** contract in the chain — all 1,452 of them, six expiries, calls and puts — at a single σ: today's ~30-day ATM implied vol, **11.832%**, the same flat vol P1 bumps in the tornado. It then reports the gap two ways: `deviation_pct` = (market − model)/market, and `deviation_vol` = the contract's own solved IV minus the flat vol. This is the same information as the P2 smile, rotated ninety degrees. P2 asks "what vol does this price imply?"; P9 asks "if I insist on one vol, what price do I get wrong, and by how much?" Both are pictures of the same failure, but the price-space version is the one that tells you whether the failure is large enough to trade against, because it is denominated in the currency the market actually quotes. The muting mirrors P7 with one deliberate difference: `within_spread` is True when the contract is quoted **and** |market − model| ≤ *half* the spread, i.e. the model price lands inside the bid–ask, whereas P7 uses the *full combined* spread of two legs because a parity trade must cross two markets. One contract, one spread; two contracts, two spreads. `test_small_deviation_inside_half_spread_is_muted` and `test_close_based_rows_are_unquoted_and_never_muted` pin both halves.

**How often the flat model is close enough to be uninteresting: almost never.** Of the 1,452 quoted contracts, the flat-vol price lands inside the bid–ask on **269 — 18.5%**. Calls do better (**206 of 774, 26.6%**) than puts (**63 of 678, 9.3%**), which is the skew again: the flat vol is calibrated at the money, calls' cheaper wing is closer to it, and the put wing is systematically dearer than any single number can reach. That 18.5% is the headline. It means that four times out of five, an honest market-maker quoting off one volatility would be quoting a price a customer could immediately trade against — not by a rounding error but beyond the spread they themselves posted. The heatmap therefore renders mostly *opaque*, not mostly muted; unlike P7, where 79.8% of cells fade out, P9's muting layer removes fewer than one cell in five, and that visual density is the finding rather than a rendering problem.

**Percent versus vol points, and why the toggle exists.** The two metrics disagree violently in the tails, and seeing where is instructive. The largest positive `deviation_pct` values are all front-expiry deep-OTM puts: at K = 540 (moneyness 0.702) the market pays **$0.055** while the flat model returns **3.7e-36** — a price of zero to thirty-five decimal places — so the deviation is **+100.0% of mid**, and it is +100% for every strike from 540 to 565. In vol points the same contract reads **+40.5**, large but finite and interpretable. The largest *negative* percentages run the other way: front-expiry calls at K = 795–808, where the market pays **$0.155** for the 807 call and the flat model asks **$0.489**, giving **−215.2% of mid** — yet in vol points that contract is only **−2.21** away from flat. Same contract, "worst on the board" by one metric and unremarkable by the other. The reason is that % of mid divides by a price that is heading to zero faster than the error is, so the ratio explodes wherever the option is worth pennies; vol points divide by nothing and stay bounded because vega, not price, is what a vol error is measured against. The lesson is the one behind question 6: price-space error is not a sound metric in the wings, and any screen that ranks "mispricings" by percentage will spend its whole time looking at contracts worth five cents. Note that vol points are not immune to bad data — the largest `deviation_vol` on the board, **+183.7 points** on the front-expiry K = 840 put, is the same class of dead deep-ITM quote that produced P7's $160 outlier.

**The error is structured, and that is the whole argument for stochastic vol.** Take the front expiry and average `deviation_vol` by moneyness band: **+19.85** vol points at 0.80–0.90, **+8.57** at 0.90–0.95, **+2.61** at 0.95–0.99, **−0.32** at 0.99–1.01, **−0.31** at 1.01–1.05. Cleanly monotone through the put wing, crossing zero exactly where the flat vol was calibrated, and slightly negative on the near call wing — the OTM call wing at 1.01–1.10 averages **−1.68** points while the OTM put wing at 0.90–0.99 averages **+6.02**. Now hold the wing fixed and vary maturity: OTM puts below 0.92 moneyness average **+20.98** vol points at 21 DTE, **+16.96** at 49, **+16.04** at 84, **+15.02** at 112, **+14.13** at 140 and **+13.16** at 203 — monotone in the other direction, the classic flattening of skew with maturity. Two monotone gradients, one in moneyness and one in time, both smooth, both the same sign every day. This is why the deviation matters more than its size. Random error of this magnitude would be a data problem you fix with better quotes. Error that is a *smooth function of the coordinates you plotted against* is a model problem: it says the price surface depends on state variables the model does not have. Constant σ generates one number; the market is quoting a surface. Getting a surface out of a model requires giving volatility somewhere to come from — make it a mean-reverting stochastic process negatively correlated with spot, as Heston does, and down-moves arrive with vol expansions, which fattens the left tail and produces a downward-sloping smile endogenously; add jumps, as Merton or Bates do, and the short-dated wings steepen exactly where our 21-DTE column is steepest and decay with maturity exactly as our +20.98 → +13.16 gradient decays. `test_skewed_market_is_rich_in_the_put_wing` is the synthetic version of this measurement: a chain built with a put-skewed vol function must come back with the put wing >50% rich in price terms and `deviation_vol` equal to the injected skew to 1e-6, while the ATM call comes back at zero.

### 2026-08-29 — Parity, the forward, and a dollar of stale spot

**Two things were wrong, and only one of them was the market's.** The Phase 5 run measured 132 tradeable parity violations out of 654 strike/expiry pairs — 20.2%, against an exit criterion of "≈ 0" — so the follow-up task was to find out why before touching a threshold. Splitting the pairs by open interest separates the causes immediately. 585 pairs have open interest on **both** legs; 69 have a leg with none, and those 69 carry **45 of the 132** violations. Those are not markets. The 2027-01-15 K = 910 put is quoted 288.50 / 293.39 with **zero** open interest while the intrinsic value is $140.65 and the call at the same strike trades at $0.42 on 4,638 contracts; our own solver inverts that put to **109.2%** implied vol. The 2026-09-18 K = 840 put is quoted at a $186.09 mid against $70.65 of intrinsic — **195.6%** implied vol — while the *same strike* at 49, 84, 112, 140 and 203 days sits at $70.9–$72.5 and 15.8–19.8% vol. A quote does not become a market by having a bid above zero and an ask; the chain filter (`bid > 0 & ask.notna()`) cannot tell the difference, and SPEC's acceptance was always "near-zero tradeable violations **on liquid strikes**", so `compute_parity` now carries `call_oi`, `put_oi` and a `liquid` flag (both legs' open interest > 0; a frame with no open-interest data at all — every Massive backfill row — counts as all-liquid, or close-based sessions would report zero pairs).

**The other cause is a dollar of stale spot, and it is visible per expiry.** Parity rearranges into a forward at *every* strike: `F = K + (C − P)·e^{rT}`, with no interpolation and no approximation, so each near-the-money strike is an independent reading of the same number and the estimate is their **median** (the follow-up entry explains why it is not the two-point interpolation this session first shipped). Discount that forward back at our own carry, `S_implied = F·e^{−(r−q)T}`, and you recover the underlying price the option quotes were actually struck against. Today's chain, against our close of **769.35**:

Our spot is EODHD's SPY **close at 16:00 ET**; SPY *options* close at **16:15 ET**. Fifteen minutes of tape is worth about a dollar on a $769 underlying, and a dollar is fatal exactly where it hurts most: in the moneyness band (0.98, 1.02] the median combined bid–ask of the two legs is **$1.07**, against **$3.2–$3.3** everywhere else. So the tightest, most liquid, most trustworthy part of the chain is the part a $1 forward error breaks first — **41** of the 87 violations among liquid pairs sat in that one band.

**Calibrating the forward removes the nuisance parameter and keeps the arbitrage.** For a fixed expiry, parity says `C − P = e^{−rT}(F − K)`: exactly **affine in K, with slope −e^{−rT}**. The dollar level of F depends on the underlying price and the funding/dividend assumption — nuisance parameters we measure separately and report as implied carry — but the *shape* does not. So the headline test now takes each expiry's F from the market's own near-the-money C − P (median over the liquid strikes within 2% of spot) and asks whether the rest of the ladder is the right straight line through it; `deviation_fwd` is the residual, and a violation of it is a genuine cross-strike inconsistency — the thing you could actually put on as a box spread. On today's chain that takes the count from **132 of 654 (20.2%, 79.8% within spread)** to **42 of the 585 liquid pairs (7.2%, 92.8% within spread)**, and the near-money band from **41 violations to 6** — and those 6 are $0.22–$0.48 gaps against $0.13–$0.22 spreads, cents rather than dollars. Two synthetic tests pin the behaviour down: `test_spot_offset_moves_raw_deviation_but_not_forward_deviation` re-prices a parity-exact chain off a spot $1 away, watches every raw deviation inside an expiry shift by the same ~$2 while every calibrated deviation stays at zero, while `test_real_violation_survives_calibration` bumps one put by $5 and requires that strike — and *only* that strike — to stay flagged, so a single bad quote cannot smear across its expiry through the ATM calibration. What the calibration does **not** test is whether the market's forward agrees with ours; that question is now answered in one place (the table above) instead of being smeared across 654 cells. "Removes the nuisance parameter" is *mostly* true rather than entirely: `deviation_fwd = (C − P) − e^{−rT}(F − K)` still carries our r in the discount factor multiplying (F − K), so moving r by 50 bp shifts the deepest out-of-the-money cell by about **$0.6 at 203 days** and **$0.15 at 49 days** (measured, not estimated) — small against the $2.8–$3.3 spreads out there, but not zero, and it is the *level* of the gap at the money that the calibration genuinely removes, not every trace of r.

**42 is not zero, and the residue is the same disease in a milder form.** 30 of the 42 sit above 1.02 moneyness — deep-ITM puts again — and their open interest is thin rather than absent: the median smaller leg holds **31** contracts and 24 of the 42 pairs have a leg under 100. The worst is 2027-03-19 K = 875, where the put is marked $111.34 on **1** contract of open interest against a $4.63 call on 7,431, a −$16.95 residual on a $3.51 spread. Layering an open-interest floor on top of the calibration now actually helps, which it never did before: 7.2% at OI ≥ 1, **4.6%** at ≥ 10, 4.8% at ≥ 100, 4.0% at ≥ 500, **3.7%** at ≥ 1,000 — where the raw spot-based measure was stuck at 12.8–16.9% at *every* one of those thresholds, because no liquidity filter can remove an error that is common to the whole expiry. American early exercise on deep-ITM puts remains the standing suspect for what is left, and it is a real effect rather than a data artefact, so the criterion is reported at 7.2% rather than tuned to zero. The heatmap changes to match: it plots the calibrated residual, mutes cells whose legs have no open interest as well as those inside the spread, and takes its colour limit from the 98th percentile of |deviation| instead of the maximum — previously the single $160.28 dead quote set a ±$160 scale and washed every real cell white (`test_outlier_does_not_flatten_the_scale`).

**Finally, why the page stopped quoting short-dated carry.** Implied carry is `ln(F/S)/T`, so a *fixed dollar* of forward error is divided by the tenor: the same ~$1 miss is 220 basis points at 21 days (0.65% against our r − q of 2.85%) and about 22 basis points at 203. Reporting carry at the ~30-day expiry, as Phase 5 shipped it, was therefore the least informative choice available — it published the largest number the same error can produce. `carry_reference` therefore restricts itself to expiries at or beyond 84 days. Its first version then took the **longest** of them, and the page read "ATM implied carry at the 203-day expiry: 3.23% vs our r − q = 2.85%". That was the wrong reduction, and this paragraph said so without acting on it: the 84-, 112- and 140-day expiries land within **3 basis points** of r − q (2.827%, 2.870%, 2.858%) while the 203-day one is **38 above** (its implied spot is $1.62 *above* our close, the opposite sign to the front months, which is its own small mystery about the March-2027 ladder). Longest is the most *stable* per dollar of forward error, but "least noisy" is not "least biased", and publishing the single reading that disagreed most with the other three is not a defensible summary of them. The branch's final review applied one level up the same argument that had just been applied to the forward itself — one reading is not an estimate, the median of many is — so `carry_reference` now returns the **median over the qualifying expiries together with their range and tenor span**, and the page reads **"ATM implied carry across the 84–203-day expiries: median 2.86% (range 2.83–3.23%) vs our r − q = 2.85%"**. The range is not decoration: printing the median alone would flatter it by hiding exactly the 40-bp disagreement this paragraph exists to record. `status.json` carries `implied_carry` (the median) beside `implied_carry_lo` / `_hi` / `_dte_min` / `_dte_max` / `_expiries`, so the file describes its own summary statistic. The published number moved **3.23% → 2.86%**, which happens to be closer to our r − q; that is a consequence of the rule, not the reason for it, and the 203-day outlier is still on the page inside the range.

### 2026-08-29 — The early-exercise residue

**The residue has a signature.** After calibrating each expiry's forward, 42 of the 585 liquid pairs still violated parity beyond the combined spread — and the shape of what was left was a signature, not noise: **41 of the 42 had a negative deviation** (C − P *below* the forward relation, i.e. the put rich against the call) and **36 were in-the-money puts**, K above our 769.35 spot. That is what American early exercise looks like. Parity is a statement about *European* options; SPY's are American, and the right to exercise an in-the-money put early is worth something the European price does not contain.

**The derivation this entry first gave was invalid, and is corrected here.** It argued: an American put is worth **at least** its intrinsic K − S, the European one is worth K·e^{−rT} − S·e^{−qT} + C, subtract, and out falls the bound. That fails twice. A premise of the form "worth at least" bounds the American premium from *below*, which is the wrong direction entirely for a *ceiling* on what early exercise can add; and the subtraction as written does not even yield the formula that was used — it yields K(1 − e^{−rT}) − S(1 − e^{−qT}) − C, with a call price still attached. The correct statement is standard and short. The rigorous ceiling on the American-minus-European **put** premium is **K·(1 − e^{−rT})**: the entire benefit of exercising early is receiving the strike now rather than at T, so the right can never be worth more than the interest earned on K over the remaining life. (Equivalently, the Kim decomposition writes that premium as ∫₀ᵀ (r·K·e^{−rt} − q·S·e^{−qt}) dt weighted by the probability of sitting in the exercise region; set those probabilities to 1 and keep only the interest leg and you have exactly K(1 − e^{−rT}).) The bound this project actually uses, **ee_bound = K·(1 − e^{−rT}) − S·(1 − e^{−qT})**, is that ceiling *less the dividends forgone* by giving up the stock — **strictly tighter** than the rigorous one. Tighter is the conservative direction: it explains away less than it is entitled to. It also forbids the sentence this entry used to carry, that a gap outside the bound "cannot be early exercise at all, no matter how the option is modelled" — that claim belongs to K(1 − e^{−rT}) and to nothing smaller. What still does *not* appear anywhere in it: no volatility, no quote, no distribution. It depends only on K, T, r, q and S, and `compute_parity` carries it on every row as `early_exercise_bound` (clipped at zero: for a short tenor and a fat q the expression goes negative, which simply means early exercise is worth nothing there).

**The counterfactual is the strongest evidence that nothing here was tuned toward a target.** Swap the tight bound for the rigorous ceiling and, on the two-point-forward frame the reviewer measured, **all 43 violations would have been "explained" — 0 unexplained; with that ceiling alone, without the spread term, 4.** (On this session's median-forward frame the same two counterfactuals read 1 and 5, the survivor being a single *call*-rich cell that no put bound can touch.) A criterion chosen to make the headline small was available, derivable, and rigorous, and we publish the tighter bound and the larger residue instead.

**And the gate the first version was missing.** `early_exercise_explained` marks a violation as accounted for when `deviation_fwd < 0` **and** `strike > spot` **and** `|deviation_fwd| ≤ ee_bound + spread`. The sign condition is not decoration — a *call* quoted too rich pushes the deviation the other way, and no amount of put early exercise can explain a positive gap (`test_wrong_sign_gap_is_never_explained`). Neither is the moneyness condition. Exercising a put means selling the stock at K; when K is *below* the market that is strictly worse than selling at the market, so the right is worth nothing to exercise and early exercise cannot be the story. But ee_bound grows with K·rT whether or not exercising is rational, so without the gate the classifier "explained" two out-of-the-money wing puts — 140d K = 540 at 0.70 moneyness, and 21d K = 671 — on strength it does not have (`test_out_of_the_money_put_gap_inside_the_bound_is_not_explained`). Adding the gate **raises** the headline, 3 → 5 on the two-point frame, which is exactly why the first version's stated reason for skipping it — that adding it would have been tuning — was backwards: tuning is what moves a number toward the answer you want. The flag stays false wherever there is no tradeable violation, so it reads as "this violation is explained", never "this row could be explained".

**It is a bound, not a price.** This is the part worth being careful about. The true early-exercise premium of an American put is a hard number that requires a model — a lattice, or a free-boundary PDE — and it depends on volatility, which is precisely the input parity was chosen to avoid. What is derived above is the *ceiling*: the value of exercising immediately and putting the strike in the bank, which is the best case for exercising and therefore an upper limit on what the right to do so can be worth. So passing the test is weak evidence and failing it is strong evidence. A gap inside the bound is *consistent with* early exercise — it is not proof that early exercise is what produced it. A gap outside the bound is *unexplained by this test*, which is weaker than it sounds: because our bound is tighter than the rigorous K(1 − e^{−rT}) ceiling, a gap can fall outside it and still be early exercise. That is the price of being conservative, and it is paid deliberately. On the stored 2026-08-28 chain the split is **36 explained, 6 unexplained**. All 36 are in-the-money puts (the gate guarantees it), and 35 of the 36 sit inside the bound *before* the spread term is added at all — the spread rescues exactly one, the 203-day K = 875 pair at −$16.95 against a $14.28 bound and a $3.51 spread. The bound scales with K·rT, so the explained population concentrates where it should: 14 of the 36 are at 203 days, 8 at 140, 8 at 112, 4 at 84 and 2 at 49. Representative cells: **84-day K = 825, −$3.53 against a $5.51 bound**; **49-day K = 800, −$1.17 against $3.10**.

**What the panel now claims, and the caveat it carries.** The exit-criterion chain reads **132 of 654 quoted pairs (20.2%) against our spot-derived forward → 42 of 585 liquid pairs (7.2%) once each expiry's forward is calibrated to the median of its own near-the-money C − P → 6 of 585 (1.03%) once the early-exercise bound is applied to the in-the-money puts**. No threshold was tuned to get there — the bound is a derived no-arbitrage quantity and whatever survives it is what gets printed — and the last correction moved the number the *wrong* way for anyone hoping to declare victory. The heatmap burns only those six at full opacity and mutes the 36 with an annotation saying why, and the stat line leads with the unexplained count while keeping both other numbers visible.

### 2026-08-29 — A ruler that does not wobble, and two lessons about being wrong in public

**The forward was estimated from two quotes.** Everything downstream of the calibration — every `deviation_fwd`, every violation count, the muting on the heatmap — hangs off one number per expiry, and until this pass that number came from interpolating C − P between the two strikes bracketing spot. A reviewer's stress test showed exactly how fragile that is: add **$1 to just the two at-the-money call quotes** of the front expiry, which is a plausible amount of staleness on a $769 underlying, and the front forward moves a full **$1.00**, forward-calibrated violations go **43 → 64** and the unexplained count **3 → 4**. A two-point estimator is the same class of fragility that this whole rework exists to remove; it was replaced with the obvious thing. Parity says F = K + (C − P)·e^{rT} at *every* strike, so the near-the-money ladder is not one reading with an interpolation — it is many independent readings of one number, and their spread is pure noise and staleness. The estimator is now the **median** of F_K over the liquid strikes within 2% of spot, falling back to the old two-point interpolation when fewer than three qualify (and, as before, to all strikes when no liquid ones bracket spot; it still never extrapolates). `implied_forward` reports `n_forward_strikes` so the reader can see how thin the estimate is: today **31, 31, 6, 6, 6, 6** — the two front expiries quote a $1 ladder, the monthlies a $5 one. Under the same stress the median forward moves **$0.06** instead of $1.00 and the violation count 42 → 45 — the three extra being the two bad quotes showing up on their own strikes, plus their immediate neighbour at K = 771, whose spread is too tight to absorb even the six cents the median did move. Flagging the bad quotes rather than absorbing them is precisely what a robust estimator should do (`test_two_bumped_atm_calls_barely_move_the_median_forward`).

**It changed almost nothing, and that is the point.** Median against two-point, expiry by expiry, the forwards differ by **$0.01 to $0.13** — the two-point value was not *wrong*, it was *unprotected*. But "almost nothing" is not "nothing", and the honest accounting is that the −$0.055 shift at 49 days moved three cells across their thresholds: the K = 775 and K = 776 in-the-money puts dropped inside their 12-cent spreads and stopped being violations, while the K = 740 pair — call rich by $0.379 against a $0.360 spread — crossed **two cents** the other way and became the only positive-deviation violation on the chain. Net: 43 → 42 violations, and one more unexplained. Three cells on a knife-edge is a fair description of what a $0.12 spread near the money buys you.

**Two of the fixes in this pass were corrections to our own reasoning, not to the code's behaviour**, and both pushed the headline the wrong way for anyone keeping score: the early-exercise derivation was invalid as written (the entry above now carries the correct one), and the classifier was missing the in-the-money gate that makes the story physically possible at all. Together they took the unexplained count from **3 to 5**, and the new forward estimator took it to **6**. The count chain, end to end, is now **132 of 654 quoted pairs → 42 of 585 liquid pairs → 6 of 585 (1.03%)**. Nothing was adjusted anywhere else to absorb the increase; the temptation to do so is the entire reason for writing this paragraph down.

**Four smaller honesty repairs on the page itself.** *(a)* The close-based stat line claimed "the heatmap shows raw deviations only" while the figure had, since the forward rework, been titling itself "against the market's own implied forward" on those very frames — and **eleven of our twelve stored sessions are close-based**, so any Massive-fallback day published a panel that contradicted itself in two places at once. The sentence now names what is plotted, and falls back to "raw" only when no expiry produced a forward, which is the same condition the figure uses. *(b)* The stat line asserted a *causal* claim — that the raw violations are "largely a stale-spot artefact" — unconditionally, including on sessions where the calibration removed nothing. It is now printed only when the calibration actually halved a double-digit raw count; otherwise the two numbers appear side by side with no story attached. *(c)* The P7 caption had drifted from the code in three ways: it named "deep in-the-money puts" where the classifier mutes put-rich gaps, it quoted the bound without the `+ spread` the code actually tests against, and it never disclosed the *limitation* of calibrating the forward — that a pure level error at the money is now absorbed and can no longer register, leaving only the shape of the ladder under test. All three are fixed, and the caption now also says the bound is the tighter of the two available. *(d)* The hot heatmap layer was still named "beyond spread" after it had come to mean liquid **and** beyond spread **and** unexplained; it is called "unexplained".

**And two layout defects that only a browser could find.** The early-exercise annotation was anchored so that it sat on top of the top row of cells and the y-axis label rather than above the plot; it is now anchored by its bottom edge above the plot area with the top margin to match. The model-vs-market panel drew a second colorbar at x = 0.46, which is *inside* the Puts subplot, over its tick labels — both subplots of a metric share zmin/zmax, so one colorbar was always sufficient; the Calls layers keep their traces (the toggle's `visible` arrays still swap four for four) and simply stop drawing a scale. Neither was visible in any assertion we had, which is the useful lesson: a figure test that reads trace data proves the numbers, never the picture.

**A unit that was wrong by 100× on the page and only on the page.** P9's second metric plots `deviation_vol`, a *fraction* of vol, under a colorbar titled "Vol points" with limits ±0.20 and a hover format of `%{z:+.1%}`. **A hand-edited YAML file could cost a session of market data.** `load_annotations` raises on a malformed entry, and `run_daily` called it *after* the entire compute stage and *before* the first write. One missing `note:` in `data/annotations.yaml` — a file SPEC deliberately designs for hand-editing, "adding a news note is a 1-line commit" — therefore aborted the run and cost that session's chain parquet, `daily_metrics` row, page and status. The project's own reasoning is sharper than that: such a gap is *consistent with* early exercise, and a stale mark of the same size fits the bound just as comfortably. The annotation now reads "36 put-rich gaps shown muted — consistent with the American early-exercise bound" and the caption says the muting is a claim about consistency, not proof. And the faint heatmap layer, which carries **every** cell — within-spread, illiquid, explained and hot — stopped being called "within spread"; on P7 it is "all pairs". None of these moved a number. All of them are places where the page said something slightly stronger than the code had earned, and the cumulative version of that is a dashboard nobody should trust.

### 2026-08-29 — Phase 6: the hedging argument, and what one round trip is worth

**The argument, derived.** Sell one straddle worth `V(S, t, σ)` and hold `H` shares against it. The position is `Π = cash + H·S − V`, and over one session its change is `dΠ = H·dS − dV + carry`. Expand `dV` to second order in the move and first order in time and vol — the only expansion Black-Scholes ever needs — and it is `dV = Δ dS + ½ Γ (dS)² + Θ dt + 𝒱 dσ + …`. Substituting, the `Δ dS` term cancels against `H·dS` the instant you set `H = Δ`. That cancellation is the whole meaning of "delta-hedged": it is a construction, not a result, and nothing about the market has to cooperate for it to hold. What survives is `dΠ = −½ Γ (dS)² − Θ dt − 𝒱 dσ + carry`. Now use the model's own theta. Black-Scholes prices an option precisely so that `Θ = −½ Γ S² σ_implied² − (the r and q carry terms the cash account and the share dividend pay back exactly)`, which is what the equation `Θ + ½σ²S²Γ + rSΔ − rV = 0` says when you read it as an accounting statement instead of a PDE. Drop the carry, which cancels by construction here, substitute, and write the day's actual squared return as `(dS/S)² = σ_realized² dt`, and the whole thing collapses to **`dΠ ≈ ½ Γ S² (σ²_implied − σ²_realized) dt`** for the short position. Theta is rent the model charges the option's owner for holding gamma; gamma against the actual squared move is what the owner pays it with. Sell an option, hedge its delta away, and you are left holding exactly one bet: that the vol you sold exceeds the vol that arrives. That is why this panel is the empirical climax and not just another chart — every other panel on this page measures a *quote*; this one measures whether acting on the quote pays.

**Which term the panel measures, and which it cannot.** The derivation drops `−𝒱 dσ`, and on real data that term is not small. The panel measures the **total**: option mark plus share hedge plus interest plus dividend, which is what a desk's book would actually print at the close. It cannot separate the gamma-versus-realized term from the vega-versus-vol-move term, because it stores marks and not the path of Greeks, and it does not pretend to. Measured on 2026-08-14, the one trade in this archive whose every single mark is a live market quote: summing `½ Γ S² (σ²_i dt − (dS/S)²)` day by day over its ten sessions predicts **+$2.88**, summing `−𝒱·Δσ` leg by leg over the same sessions gives **+$2.59**, and the trade's actual mark-to-market P&L is **+$5.45**. The two terms reconstruct the answer to two cents — and roughly **47% of what the panel plots as "gamma P&L" is not gamma at all**, it is a short position profiting from ATM vol falling from 12.55% at entry to 10.90% by 2026-08-28. The P8b caption's phrase "plus the vol mark-to-market the model assumes away" is worth $2.59 on this archive, and that is a bigger share of the headline than the words suggest. Correlation between the daily gamma prediction and the actual daily P&L on that trade's ten market-marked sessions is **0.65**; on the July trade's six it is **0.39**. Neither is a validation of anything. What *is* nearly perfect is the 2024 trade at **0.98** — and that number is a tautology, not evidence: twelve of its thirteen marks are our own `bs_price` at frozen entry vol, so the gamma formula is being checked against a price the gamma formula generated. The one place the model looks flawless is the one place no market was consulted.

**A sign convention that differs from SPEC on purpose.** `SPEC.md:323` writes the result as `½ Γ S² (σ²_realized − σ²_implied) dt`, the canonical **long**-option form: buy gamma, and you profit when the world moves more than you paid for. The P8b caption writes `½ Γ S² (σ²_implied − σ²_realized) dt` because this panel is **short** a straddle, and says "a delta-hedged short option" in the same sentence. Both are the same statement once the position's sign is applied. Recording it here so the divergence reads as deliberate rather than as a transcription error by whoever reads the two side by side.

**The P&L identity, and why it is a test rather than a comment.** `pv_t − pv_{t−1} == pnl_interest + pnl_dividend + pnl_hedge + pnl_option − pnl_cost` is an **identity**, not an approximation: `pv` is *defined* as `cash + H·S − V`, and `cash` is advanced by exactly those five flows, so the two sides are the same algebra written twice. The test asserts it at `atol=1e-10`; the worst residual across all 34 stepped sessions in this run is **1.08e-13**, which is float noise on numbers of order 10. Writing that as a comment — "the daily P&L decomposes into these five pieces" — would have been worth nothing, because the interesting failure is silent. Re-hedge at one price while measuring the hedge P&L at another, or buy shares and forget to debit the cash for them, and every number on the page stays plausible: the cumulative line still slopes, the histogram still has width, the scatter still has dots. Nothing looks wrong; the P&L is just quietly not the P&L of any position anyone could hold. An exact identity is the only kind of check that catches that class of bug, and it is available here precisely because the simulation is a closed accounting system with no external truth to compare against. Where the code cannot construct such an identity it does not get one — which is why this branch has exactly one assertion of this kind and not five decorative ones.

Because that is a Saturday the snapshot is correctly labelled with the last settled session, **2026-08-28** — the documented behaviour, same as Phase 5. It fetched a **yfinance** chain, stored **1,452** rows, solved IVs at **98.83%** convergence, and rendered **eleven** panels (P1–P7, P8a/P8b/P8c, P9) into a **1.71 MB** self-contained page with the Plotly bundle inlined, no `<script src=`, no CDN link and no web font. SPEC §5's Phase 6 exit criterion is the one being claimed: first simulated trade running mark-to-market daily, scatter renders, sparse is fine. That is met. The panel heals itself as chain history accumulates and needs no code change to do it.

**Finding 1 — the entry rule and the tenor cannot both be satisfied, and neither was bent.** SPEC §3 P8 says "on the first trading day each month, sell one ~30-DTE ATM straddle". SPEC §2.2's chain filter stores **monthly expiries only**. Those two sentences are individually reasonable and jointly impossible: read from the first session of a month, the monthly ladder offers the *current* month's third Friday at roughly 16–18 days or the *next* one at roughly 45, and never anything near 30. Measured on this archive, printing the actual ladder each entry session saw:

```
2024-09-03  dtes=[17, 45, 73, 108, 136, 199]   nearest-to-30 = 17,  error 13
2026-06-01  dtes=[46, 81, 109, 137, 172, 200]  nearest-to-30 = 46,  error 16  -> SKIPPED
2026-07-01  dtes=[16, 51, 79, 107, 142, 170]   nearest-to-30 = 16,  error 14
2026-08-14  dtes=[ 7, 35, 63,  98, 126, 154]   nearest-to-30 = 35,  error  5
```

`MAX_ENTRY_DTE_ERROR = 15` was deliberately **not** widened to 16 to rescue June, and not tightened to 10 to make the sample look cleaner. Either move is tuning a threshold until the data agrees with you, and both would have been invisible on the published page. The tenor is **disclosed** instead: `dte_at_entry_min/max` reach the stat line ("sold 16–35 days from expiry") and `status.json`, and both P8 captions state the rule and why the nearest monthly is not 30. This matters more than a footnote, because a delta-hedged straddle's round-trip P&L scales roughly as `S·φ(0)·√T·(σ_i − σ_r)`: at *identical* edge a 45-day trade earns about **1.6×** the dollars of a 17-day one. Left unstated, that is pure vertical spread in the scatter — and a reader would have charged it to hedging error, which is the one thing the panel exists to measure.

**Finding 2 — June 2026 is missing because of Juneteenth, and the gap was parked in this log four phases ago.** June 2026's third Friday is **2026-06-19**, which is the Juneteenth holiday, so the exchange shifted that monthly contract to the preceding **Thursday, 2026-06-18**. `is_monthly_expiry` in `src/data/filters.py` is a literal reading of "third Friday" — `d.weekday() == 4 and 15 <= d.day <= 21` — so it does not recognise the shifted contract, and the June expiry is absent from **every** stored June chain: 2026-06-01's ladder starts at 2026-07-17. The nearest-to-30 pick is therefore 46 days, the error is 16, and the whole month drops out. The holiday-shifted-monthly hole was already written down during Phase 2 as a known limitation of the expiry filter and consciously left alone as low-cost. It has now cost a real trade in a downstream panel two phases later, which is the general lesson worth more than the specific bug: **a parked gap in a data filter does not stay a data-layer problem.** It surfaces as a missing dot in an analytics panel long after anyone remembers parking it, and — worse — it surfaces *silently*. `simulate` returns a `skipped` count precisely so it cannot: the stat line publishes "1 month in the archive produced no trade", so the absence is a stated number rather than a hole in a chart nobody counts.

**Finding 3 — a threshold that was structurally unreachable, found only by running the finished code against real data.** This is the finding of the phase, and it is the one that would have shipped a permanently broken panel with a green suite behind it. `MIN_MARKET_MARK_SHARE = 0.7` exists to keep a trade off the money chart when its P&L is mostly our own model talking to itself. Reasonable rule. The original implementation measured it as `n_market_marks / n_days` — every session of the trade's life in the denominator. But `chain_filter.dte_min = 7` means a stored chain **never carries an expiry closer than 7 days**, so over its final week every trade's own expiry is absent from every chain in the archive *by design*. Combine that with Finding 1 — the entry rule systematically produces 16–18 day tenors — and the arithmetic is fatal: an 11-session trade spends 4 sessions inside the structural window, so the **maximum attainable** blended share is **7/11 = 0.636**, against a bar of 0.70. Not "usually falls short". **Cannot reach.** Every trade the entry rule produces at its characteristic tenor was permanently `sparse`, the scatter could never populate, and *no amount of backfilling would ever have fixed it* — the panel would have sat empty forever while the stat line explained, correctly and uselessly, that it was waiting for more data. Nothing in a unit test could see this: every test asserted that the share was computed the way the code computed it, and every fixture was written by someone who already believed the rule. What found it was running the finished pipeline against the real archive and asking why a trade that had visibly settled was still off the chart. I then tried the obvious thing first and it disproved itself: I backfilled the chains for **2026-07-14, 07-15 and 07-16** specifically to raise July's share, and the share **did not move one point** — because those chain files already existed and correctly omit the 07-17 expiry, which is inside `dte_min`. Missing data was not the problem; the ruler was. **The fix was to correct the measurement, not to move the bar.** `mark_source` now splits into `model_structural` (the session is inside `dte_min`, so no chain here or anywhere could have quoted this straddle) and `model_gap` (the archive genuinely lacks a usable quote where one could have existed — file missing, or present but the strike lost a leg to an unconverged IV or to the moneyness filter). Only `model_gap` belongs in the denominator, because only it says anything about how well the archive covers this trade. `MIN_MARKET_MARK_SHARE` stayed at 0.70, untouched. July flips to **7/7 = 1.000** and becomes the archive's first settled trade; 2024-09-03 stays at **1/9 = 0.111** and remains sparse — which is exactly the discrimination the threshold was written to make, and proof the fix did not simply lower the bar in disguise. Both shares are published side by side (`hedge_market_mark_share: 0.5294`, `hedge_quotable_mark_share: 0.6923`) with the counts that make them, because reporting only the quotable share would under-report how much of the P&L is modelled, and reporting only the blended one would make a plottable trade look unqualified. Two lessons. **(a)** A quality gate must measure something the system can actually achieve; a threshold on a quantity with a structural ceiling below it is not a strict standard, it is a permanently closed door, and it fails in the direction that looks like caution. **(b)** The bug lived in the gap between two config values in two different sections of one YAML file — `hedge_sim.entry_dte: 30` and `chain_filter.dte_min: 7` — neither of which is wrong, and whose interaction no test and no reviewer had a reason to compute. Only the real run put them in the same sentence.

**Finding 4 — "sold at the mid" is false for 50 of the 51 stored sessions.** SPEC §3 P8 says the straddle is sold "at mid". `compute_chain_iv` sets `price_used = mid` only for rows from a live source and `close` otherwise, and this archive is **50 `massive-backfill` sessions to 1 `yfinance`** — including the entry sessions of *all three* trades. Every trade on this page was therefore entered at an official close, not a quote mid, and marked the same way on every backfilled session. The page states the routing rule rather than the aspiration: "marked at each session's stored price: the quote mid on live sessions, the official close on backfilled (close-based) ones". Two consequences worth naming. It removes any bid-ask realism from the entry — a close is a single print, not a two-sided market, so "sold at the mid" would have been *generous* in a way this simulation cannot even quantify, on top of `transaction_cost_bps: 0`. And `SPEC.md:309-313` still says "at mid" and was **deliberately left unedited**. The spec is the authority the plan argues from; quietly rewriting it to match what the code turned out to do inverts that relationship and destroys the only record of the divergence. It is recorded here as a known divergence and a one-line follow-up, which is what a spec conflict is supposed to cost.

**Five things in the numbers that surprised me.** *(a) The July trade gave back half its P&L on the single day it settled.* It peaked at **+$5.53** on 2026-07-16 and closed at **+$2.77** on 2026-07-17 — a **−$2.76** session, the largest single-day loss anywhere in the sample, larger in magnitude than any of the 2024 trade's days during a genuinely volatile fortnight. The mechanism is discrete hedging at its purest: the final hedge was set on 07-16 at +0.726 shares using a *model* delta at frozen 11.2% vol (07-16 is inside `dte_min`, so there was no quote), SPY then fell 750.72 → 743.29, and the hedge lost **$5.40** while the short option gained only **$2.68**. One day, at the one moment the position carries the most gamma and the hedge is the stalest, and 50% of a two-week round trip is gone. If someone wants a single number for what "you cannot hedge continuously" costs, it is that one. *(b) The gamma and vega terms are the same order of magnitude.* I expected the vol mark-to-market to be a correction; on the August trade it is **$2.59 against $2.88** — very nearly half the P&L of the panel's flagship number. *(c) A "delta-neutral" position on one share traded nearly one and a half shares.* The 2024 trade's hedge ran from **−0.459** to **+0.999** shares over thirteen sessions. Neutral is a property of an instant, not of a position, and the turnover it takes to maintain is where `transaction_cost_bps` will bite the moment it is turned on. *(d) The delta hedge really does turn direction into variance.* SPY rose **552.08 → 568.25, +2.93%**, over the 2024 trade's life — far outside the straddle's $15.64 breakeven, so a naked short would have lost. Hedged, it made **+$2.26**, because 13.48% realized came in under 16.46% sold. That is the argument at the top of this entry doing exactly what it says on real 2024 data, and it is the most convincing thing on the page even though the trade is `sparse` and correctly kept off the scatter. *(e) The histogram filter widened the distribution rather than narrowing it — the opposite of the prediction, and of the earlier measurement.* `build_hedge_histogram_figure` excludes model-marked sessions on the argument that a frozen-vol model price is Black-Scholes-conforming by construction and would flatter the very width the panel exists to measure. Measured on this run: all 34 stepped sessions have sd **$0.82**; the 18 the histogram keeps have sd **$0.92**; the 16 it drops have sd **$0.71**. So the filter *widens* the picture by $0.10, because the excluded model days are the calm middles of two trades while the single largest move in the entire sample — the −$2.76 July settlement — is a settlement mark and survives. An earlier measurement on a smaller archive found the reverse (a narrowing, $0.68 → $0.57) and attributed it to the 2024 trade's model days carrying the tails; on the archive as it stands today that no longer holds. Both directions are consistent with the filter's justification, which is worth being clear about: the argument for excluding those days is that they cannot contain a vol mark-to-market, and that argument does not depend on which way the sd happens to move on a sample of eighteen. The number to watch is not the direction, it is that a *conceptual* filter on a two-dozen-point sample can move the headline either way — which is one more reason the panel says eighteen sessions instead of drawing conclusions from them.
